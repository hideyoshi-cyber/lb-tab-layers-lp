Why does video production
cost so much?

Nov 25, 2026 · By Hideyoshi Saimoto, CEO of Liquid Block Inc. · 8 min read

PostShareShare

The price of a video depends on which stages (sections) are involved and how many people work on each. If a quote is higher than you expected, the quickest step is to ask, “What can we do within this budget?”, after you have agreed on the basics: the purpose, where it will be used, who decides and the deadline. A vague brief gets a vague quote. The best way to close the gap is a first meeting where both sides bring reference videos.

People often say video production pricing is hard to understand. If you have ever opened a quote and thought “that’s more than I expected”, you are not alone. As a video and motion graphics studio in Kobe, Japan, we want to explain how the price is actually decided, and what you can do to keep the gap between your expectations and the quote small.

What a video quote is made of: which stages, and how many people

People often say filming is expensive. But there is no fixed price on “filming” as such. The price comes from which sections are involved, and how many people work in each.

A video with a shoot, for example, can involve a director, a camera operator, lighting, a stylist, on-screen talent, a studio, an editor and CG artists. When a quote looks high, look at which sections it covers and how many people it assumes. The reason usually becomes clear.

SectionWho is involved (examples)
Planning and storyboardDirector
ShootCamera, lighting, stylist, talent, studio
EditingEditor
CG and compositingCG artists

Drop the shoot, use fewer people on camera, skip the CG: fewer sections and fewer people mean a lower price. Put the other way round, until you decide what to make, nobody can know how many people it needs.

Why a vague brief gets a vague quote

“One company introduction video, please.” A request this broad can only get a broad quote. The studio doesn’t know what the video will be, so it has to estimate the people it might need.

Think of buying a car. If you haven’t decided whether you want a sports car or a station wagon, the conversation about price has started too early. Talk about money before you know what you want, and the numbers in your head and in the studio’s head will almost always differ.

This is not the client’s fault. Video is hard to picture before it exists. When you can’t picture it, the brief naturally gets vaguer. That is exactly why you need a step that gets everyone picturing the same thing.

Don’t ask an udon shop for a cake

Starting a project without deciding what to make is like deciding to eat out, but walking into a restaurant with no idea what you want to eat.

And here is what often happens next. You walk into an udon shop (a Japanese noodle restaurant that makes thick wheat noodles) and start asking about a cake. The owner thinks, “Sure, it’s all wheat flour, but the cooking is completely different. This is an udon shop.” Still, the owner takes the order. And what you get is “the udon shop’s cake”.

Video studios have their strengths and their ways of working too. Some are best at live action, some at CG, some at motion graphics. Agree on what you want to make first, and the right studio, the right people and the price all follow.

If you have a budget, ask “What can we do within this budget?”

If your budget is fixed, the fastest route is to ask what can be done within it. The studio can then plan how many people it can put in which sections, and make a proposal that fits.

But first, a few basics need to be settled, so the proposal has something to stand on:

  • What the video is for (who watches it, and what should they do afterwards)
  • Where it will be used, and roughly how long it is
  • Who makes the final decision
  • The deadline, and how many review rounds

With those in place, the studio can say something concrete, such as “for this budget, no shoot: we’ll use your existing photos with motion graphics.” What to put in a brief is covered in how to brief a video production company and give video feedback.

Start with a meeting where both sides bring reference videos

Our strongest recommendation: before anything else, hold one meeting where the client and the studio both bring reference videos.

Words like “stylish” or “fast-paced” bring a different video to mind for every person. With reference videos on the screen, the conversation has something solid to point at, and the gap in expectations shrinks fast. If you already have a reference video, just bringing it is enough. The studio will bring examples it thinks are close too.

When you share them, try to say what about each video is the reference: the colors, the pace, the way text appears, the feel of the people on screen. Once everyone agrees on “this scene, in this video, this feeling”, the sections and the number of people become clear, and so does the reasoning behind the quote.

Why revisions can raise the cost: video is built like a house

There is one more reason quotes grow: revisions.

Making a video is like building a house. First there is the land. The land comes with rules about what you may build on it, and the foundation is laid to fit those rules. Then the floor goes down and the pillars go up. The walls are finished before the roof goes on. Then the utilities, then the interior, and finally it is a house. Then you bring in the furniture, and someone can live there.

A video stacks up the same way: planning, shooting, editing, CG and finishing, each stage built on the one before. So if a comment about the “walls” comes up now, it means going back to the walls stage and redoing the work from there. Ask to change the walls while the interior is going in, and everything after the walls has to be redone. How far back a revision goes is what changes the cost.

Example: changing a night scene to daytime

Say a video was planned and built around a night scene, and a revision asks for it to be daytime instead. It is one sentence to write, but for the studio it is a big step back.

The look that worked because it was night, built on light, disappears in daylight: city lights, beams of light, text glowing out of the dark. The ground that look was standing on is gone, and a different approach has to be worked out from scratch. In house terms, it is a revision that goes back close to the foundation.

Before you send revisions, ask how far back you can still go

Giving a revision without knowing how far back it reaches is a common cause of friction. To the client it is “a one-line change”. To the studio it can be close to rebuilding.

So before you send revisions, ask the studio, “At this stage, how far back can we still change things?”, and decide how to phrase your changes from there. Studios can help too, by saying at each milestone, “from here on, we can only go back this far.” Agreeing on the picture at the very start is still the best way to avoid big revisions later.

When you do send revisions, say which video, what second, where and how, and send them together in one round.

Frequently asked questions

Q. How is the price of video production decided?

By which sections are involved (planning, shooting, editing, CG and so on) and how many people work on each. Filming looks expensive because it brings in many people and sections at once.

Q. What should I do if a video quote is over my budget?

Ask the studio, “What can we do within this budget?” Settle the purpose, where the video will be used, its length, who decides and the deadline first, so the studio can make a concrete proposal.

Q. How can I keep the quote close to what I expected?

Hold a first meeting where you and the studio both bring reference videos, and say what about each one is the reference. Once you agree on what to make, the sections and people needed become clear.

Q. Why do revisions make a video more expensive?

A video is built like a house, each stage on top of the one before. A change to an early stage, like the walls, means redoing everything after it. Before sending revisions, ask the studio how far back you can still change things at this stage.
