Why we hoard tabs (and how to stop without losing anything)

Oct 28, 2026 · Liquid Block Inc. · 6 min read

PostShareShare

Most tab hoarding comes from one feeling: closing a tab feels like losing it. Open tabs become a to-do list, a reminder and a safety net all at once. You stop hoarding not by willpower, but by giving every tab a safe place to go — so closing it costs nothing.

Look at your tab bar right now. How many of those tabs have you looked at today? If you are like most people with a crowded browser, the honest answer is “a few.” The rest are there just in case.

Tab hoarding isn’t a character flaw. It is a reasonable response to how browsers work. Once you see why it happens, it becomes much easier to change.

Why we keep tabs open

  • Fear of losing it. You found something useful after twenty minutes of searching. Closing the tab means you might never find it again. History is hard to search, and bookmarks feel like a place where links go to be forgotten.
  • Tabs as a to-do list. An open tab is a reminder: reply to this, read that, buy this later. Closing it feels like dropping the task.
  • Unfinished business. We tend to keep thinking about things we have started and not finished. A half-read article stays on our mind, and its tab stays open.
  • The “I’ll read it later” promise. Saving something for later feels like progress, even when later never comes.
  • Decision fatigue. Deciding where each tab belongs is work. Leaving it open postpones the decision.

What it costs

The obvious cost is speed: every open tab holds memory, and a few dozen heavy ones can slow down the whole computer. (To see which tabs cost the most, read how to check Chrome memory usage per tab.)

The less obvious cost is attention. A row of tiny tabs is a row of small, unfinished tasks staring back at you. Every time you hunt for the one you need, you pass twenty you don’t. That is time spent looking instead of thinking.

Signs your tabs have turned into a hoard

  • The tab icons are too narrow to show anything, so you can’t tell what is what.
  • You open a page you already have open, because searching the tab bar takes longer.
  • You feel a small jolt of worry when Chrome asks to restart for an update.
  • Some tabs have been open for weeks and you are not sure why.
  • You keep a second window “for later,” and it has become a third.

If two or more of these sound familiar, the tabs are no longer working for you. You are working for them.

Why “just close them” doesn’t work

The usual advice is to declare tab bankruptcy: close everything and start fresh. It feels great for a day. Then the tabs come back, because nothing has changed about the reason you kept them. The fear of losing a tab is still there, so the next useful page stays open too.

What works is removing the fear. If closing a tab is as safe as keeping it open, there is no reason to keep it open.

Five steps to stop hoarding tabs

1. Give every kind of tab a home

Make a handful of groups that match your life, not your browser: one per project, plus “Read later” and “Personal.” In LB TAB Layers, each group is a Chrome bookmark folder in the side panel, so saving a tab there keeps it as an ordinary bookmark.

2. Sweep the strays

Open tabs that aren’t in any group are listed under Solo Tabs at the bottom of the panel. That list is your hoard, made visible. If you can’t face sorting them one by one, use Group all into a new group to put them all in one place, then close them. Sort that group later, when you have five minutes.

3. Write down why you kept it

Half of hoarding is not remembering why a tab mattered. Right-click a saved tab and choose Edit note: “compare prices before Friday,” “the chart for the report.” With the reason written down, the tab no longer needs to stay open to remind you. More in notes.

4. Close by group, not by tab

Closing tabs one at a time means forty small decisions. Pressing ■ on a group closes all of its tabs at once, and they stay saved. When you need them again, ▶ opens the whole set. That turns “Do I still need this?” into “Am I working on this right now?” — a much easier question. See close all tabs in Chrome for shortcuts.

5. Let finished things go — gently

When a project is done, right-click its group and choose Archive. It disappears from your main list but stays in your bookmarks, and you can Restore it later. Archiving is a middle step between keeping and deleting, which is exactly what a hoarder needs.

What about Chrome’s own tools?

Chrome has a few built-in ways to put tabs aside, and they are worth knowing. The Reading list (in the side panel) is good for articles you will read once. Bookmark all tabs (Ctrl+Shift+D, or ⌘+Shift+D on Mac) saves every open tab into one folder before you close them. Saved tab groups keep a set of tabs on the bookmarks bar.

Each of these helps with part of the problem. What hoarders usually miss is a single place where saved tabs are sorted by what they are for, with the reason written next to them, and where a whole set can be closed and reopened in one click. That is the gap a tab manager fills — see Chrome tab groups vs. a tab manager for the comparison.

A weekly five-minute review

  1. Open the “Read later” group. Read one thing, delete two, keep the rest.
  2. Check Solo Tabs. Save or close anything left there.
  3. Archive any group you haven’t opened in a month.

The goal isn’t zero tabs. It is a tab bar that holds only what you are working on now — and the calm of knowing everything else is saved.

Frequently asked questions

Q. Why do I keep so many tabs open?

Usually because closing a tab feels like losing it. Open tabs also work as reminders and to-do items. Once each tab has a safe place to be saved, keeping it open stops being necessary.

Q. Is it bad to have a lot of tabs open?

It can slow down your computer, since every tab uses memory, and it makes the tab you need harder to find. Saving tabs to groups and closing them solves both.

Q. How do I close all my tabs without losing them?

Save them first. In LB TAB Layers, use Group all into a new group under Solo Tabs, or save tabs into your own groups, then press ■ to close the group. The tabs are kept as Chrome bookmarks and ▶ opens them again.
